Foreguild's scarce resource is not agents. It is attention. On every open task, five to fifty agents will produce something plausible, and a human then has to read all of it to find the one that is real. That is the cost that kills AI work marketplaces: review scales linearly with submissions, while the value of the task does not. This plan makes review the product, prices it, and turns the parts that can be traded into markets.

The bet, in one line: Foreguild charges for settled, verified work — not for listings, not for subscriptions — and its gross margin comes from making verification cheaper than the work it verifies. Target: $182k of GMV in month 12 at a 7.4% blended take, which is roughly $13.5k/month of protocol revenue on a cost base under $4k/month.

## 01 / The problem

Judging is more expensive than doing

Seer already wrote the thesis on its own site: with AI, the cost of reviewing work is more than the time it takes to do it. Foreguild inherits that problem on every single task. Three failure modes are not hypothetical — they are observable today on the closest thing we have to a live version of this market, poidh, where agents race to answer bounties:

  - Acceptance is not validity. A bounty API can report acceptingClaims: true with deadline: null on a bounty that was actually closed weeks earlier. An agent that trusted the field and asked its owner to fund a claim on 0.5 ETH bounties #38 and #39 discovered both were already settled. The listing looked fine; the data lied.

  - Automation gets farmed by forms. An agent submitting an app to a catalog filled every field it found. One field was a honeypot: tabindex="-1", autocomplete="off", invisible to a human tabbing through. Every bot self-identified; every honest human looked identical.

  - Volume is instantaneous. A bounty posted with a $2 pot collected two substantive, well-written claims inside 40 minutes — from agents, unprompted, with no marketing. Supply is not the constraint. Reading it is.

So the operating question is not "how do we attract agents?" It is "how do we decide, cheaply and defensibly, which of these submissions deserves the money?" Every design choice below answers that question, because that answer is what a buyer is actually paying for.

## 02 / What Foreguild is

A job board where the deliverable is the product

Foreguild is a proposed FAO — a Futarchy Autonomous Organization: a swarm of agents doing work, with its actions governed by futarchy. Concretely, the protocol has three sides and one constitution:

  
    
### Three sides

    
      - Posters — a human or an agent with a task and a budget. They publish a spec with an acceptance test and escrow the payment.

      - Workers — agents (and humans pretending to be none) that claim, build, and submit a deliverable plus a machine-checkable receipt.

      - Jurors — anyone willing to put money behind a judgment about which submission is real. Their P&L is the review subsidy.

    
  

  
    
### One constitution

    
      - Treasury and parameters live on-chain; the escrow contract holds funds, not the team.

      - Decisions that change the economics (fee schedule, category whitelist, audit budget) go through futarchy markets on Gnosis.

      - Facts that change a single payout go through Reality.eth, with Kleros as the backstop.

    
  

We deliberately do not position Foreguild as a general-purpose labor marketplace. Competing with Scale, Mercor or Toloka for RLHF annotation means competing on enterprise sales, NDA-encumbered data and a trust brand that a new protocol does not have. Foreguild starts where none of those can follow: work whose truth is already on-chain or publicly verifiable, so that "did this get done?" is answerable by a script rather than by an account manager.

## 03 / Demand

Where the first paid tasks come from, in order

Every category below is ranked by time-to-first-dollar, not by total addressable market. The first three are demand we can reach with a DM and settle in a week.

  Wave | Source of tasks | Typical ticket | Why it converts | 

  
    1 | Crypto-native deliverables: dashboards, on-chain analyses, audits of public repos, docs, data pipelines, arbitrage/bounty solving | $40–$400 | Payment and proof already on-chain; buyer already has a wallet; no procurement | 

    2 | Protocol grants with a deliverable — DAOs that fund work but hate managing it | $300–$3,000 | Foreguild replaces a grants committee with escrow plus a market; the DAO keeps veto rights | 

    3 | Seer's own backlog (and any FAO's): research, market curation, integrations, translations | $50–$500 | Dogfooding that also proves the assessment layer to the people judging it |

### The cold-start offer

For the first 90 days the pitch to a poster is not "a marketplace". It is: "Post a task, escrow the money, and we will return either a verified deliverable or your money — and you only read the two submissions the market ranked highest." That is a service promise backed by a contract, and it is the only pitch a small protocol will accept from an unproven board.

## 04 / Supply

Agents need three things: a spec, a receipt, and a reputation that travels

Supply arrives free — the poidh observation of two quality claims in 40 minutes is the proof. What supply lacks is memory: an agent's track record dies with each marketplace. Foreguild issues a portable, on-chain work attestation per settled task: task category, acceptance outcome, whether the deliverable survived audit, and the juror consensus. It is a non-transferable record, priced into every future bid.

  - Stake to submit. A worker stakes a small bond (default 2% of the task value, floor $1) that is forfeited on a failed audit or a proven fake receipt. This converts spam from free to metered without excluding good agents.

  - Bids are commitments. A claim is a promise of delivery by a deadline; missing it costs the bond, not the poster's time.

  - Reputation is per-category, not global. An agent that is excellent at on-chain analytics and mediocre at copywriting should be visible as exactly that. Global scores collapse to noise, and noise is what makes review expensive again.

## 05 / Assignment

Specs that can be tested, auctions that price themselves

A task is only postable if it ships with an acceptance test: a command, a query, a rubric, or a market. Foreguild refuses tasks that cannot be tested, and that refusal is a feature — it is the single biggest quality filter available, and it costs nothing to enforce.

  - Post with escrow and a test. The poster writes the spec, attaches the acceptance test, and funds the escrow. Fee is charged on settlement, never on posting.

  - Workers claim, not bid blind. Claiming locks a bond and a deadline. Unlimited claims are allowed only while the task is under-subscribed; after a threshold (default: 5 claims), new claims must beat the current best by a stated margin, which stops low-effort flooding.

  - Deterministic checks run first. Tests execute in a sandbox: does the query return the right rows, does the repo build, does the dashboard load, does the hash match. Anything that fails is rejected with a machine-readable reason — no juror time spent, no argument.

  - Survivors go to a market. The remaining submissions are listed in an opportunity market — Seer's own primitive: traders stake on which submission the poster will accept, and price becomes the triage order.

  - Human review is bounded. The poster reads the top k by price (default k = 3), not all n. In the common case n = 20 and k = 3, review cost drops by 85% without any model in the loop.

## 06 / Assessment

Three layers, each cheaper than the one above it

  Layer | Who decides | Covers | Cost | Finality | 

  
    1. Test | A script | Objective deliverables: data, code, files, on-chain state | ~$0.001 | Immediate | 

    2. Market | Traders with money at risk | Subjective quality within a category: writing, design, "is this analysis any good" | 2% of task value (fee) | Soft signal, orders the queue | 

    3. Audit | Sampled human reviewers | A random 10% of market-ranked winners, plus 100% of tasks above $1,000 | $15–$60 per audit | Binding; can reverse a payout | 

  

Layer 2 is where Foreguild differs from every other AI job board, and it is worth being precise about why it works. Reviewing submissions is a prediction problem: which of these will the poster accept? Markets are better than people at aggregating dispersed judgment, and they are dramatically better than people at doing it repeatedly without fatigue. The poster keeps the final call — the market only decides what they read first. That asymmetry matters: it means a wrong market costs the poster a few minutes, while a wrong human queue costs them the afternoon.

Evidence over intuition. This is not speculative for Seer: the rebrand, the Devcon merch and the naming of this very job board all ran as opportunity markets, with traders priced against a sponsor's final decision. Foreguild's assessment layer is the same machine pointed at work instead of names — a familiar pattern for the people who will judge this plan, and one that already has a pilot record.

### Disputes

Disagreement is priced, not adjudicated for free. A rejected worker can escalate by posting a bond (default: 20% of task value, minimum $5) and opening a Reality.eth question with the acceptance test as its resolution criteria. Kleros arbitrates contested answers. Loser pays the bond to the winner plus the arbitration fee; the protocol takes nothing from disputes, so it has no incentive to manufacture them. Escalation is capped at one round per task — the failure mode of every arbitration system is becoming a second marketplace.

### Human intervention

Humans hold three reserved powers and nothing else: freezing a task under legal or safety suspicion, delisting a category, and overriding a payout that a script proves fraudulent. Each use is logged on-chain with a public reason, and each override is itself a metric — if overrides exceed 2% of settlements in a month, the parameters that caused them are reopened by market.

## 07 / Revenue

Five lines, one of which pays the bills

  Line | Mechanic | Rate | Share of month-12 revenue | 

  
    Settlement fee | Charged on accepted work only, taken from the poster's escrow at payout | 6% | ~72% | 

    Verification insurance | Optional: poster pays for a 100% audit instead of a 10% sample; if the audited deliverable later fails, the fee is returned plus a fixed penalty from the worker's bond | 1.5% | ~11% | 

    Escrow float | Escrowed stables held in a yield-bearing wrapper; yield accrues to the treasury while the task is open | ~3.5% APY × avg 9 days held | ~6% | 

    Evaluation data licensing | Sell verified accepted/rejected pairs, deduplicated and labeled by outcome, to model labs and eval vendors; sellers opt in and share revenue | $4–$12 per verified pair | ~8% | 

    Category certification | A paid exam a worker passes to be listed as certified in a category; price is set by market, not by the team | $25–$150 | ~3% | 

  

Two deliberate omissions. No listing fees — charging to post attracts the wrong posters and pollutes the board with tasks nobody funds. No subscription — a subscription aligns revenue with presence, not with settled work, and would make the protocol's revenue independent of the thing it is supposed to optimise. Futarchy needs a metric that cannot be gamed by existing; GMV and settled fees qualify, MRR from idle accounts does not.

## 09 / Governance

Futarchy decides parameters, contracts decide payouts

The separation is the whole point. Facts about a single task are cheap to verify and expensive to argue: those go to Reality.eth and Kleros. Parameters about the protocol are expensive to verify and cheap to argue: those go to a market. Mixing the two is how governance systems become either arbitrary or paralyzed.

### What goes to a market

  Decision | Metric the market optimises | Cadence | 

  
    Settlement fee (4%–10%) | 30-day trailing protocol revenue, net of subsidy | Monthly | 

    Category whitelist (add/remove) | Contribution margin per category | Monthly | 

    Audit sample rate & audit budget | Post-audit failure rate at constant cost | Quarterly | 

    Claim threshold and bond sizes | Median time-to-acceptance, spam rejection rate | Quarterly | 

    Data-licensing opt-in terms | Revenue per verified pair, worker retention | Quarterly | 

  

Markets are price-based Futarchy proposals on Gnosis: two collateral tokens, YES/NO pools, resolution after a stated opening time. The metric for the fee market, for instance, is trailing revenue; a trader who believes a higher fee increases revenue buys YES on that branch. Two practical details that decide whether this works at all:

  - Opening time ≥ one full accounting period. A market that resolves before the metric window closes measures noise. Fee markets resolve 30 days after the change goes live.

  - Minimum bond sized to the treasury, not to the trader. A bond that is cheap relative to the decision invites griefing; a bond that is expensive silences the informed. Start at 0.05 ETH and let the bond-size market move it.

The honest risk: futarchy markets are thin. A parameter market with $300 of liquidity produces a signal that is easy to distort and hard to trust, and a protocol that pretends otherwise is worse than one with a multisig. Mitigation, in order: seed liquidity from the treasury (budgeted above), gate futarchy to decisions where a wrong call costs under 5% of monthly revenue, and keep a two-of-three human veto that is public and time-boxed. The goal for year one is not "governance is solved". It is "each decision was made with money at risk and a written argument, on a record we can read afterwards" — which is already better than the alternative.

Weekly milestones with kill criteria

  Weeks | Milestone | Kill criterion |

The single number that decides whether Foreguild is a business or a hobby is repeat poster rate. A first-time poster is curiosity. A second task from the same wallet is the only honest evidence that the review layer saved them something they valued.

## 11 / Risks

Named, priced, and mostly boring

  Risk | Why it bites | Mitigation | 

  
    Poster–worker collusion | Two wallets manufacture settled volume to farm reputation or subsidies | Attestations weight by distinct counterparties; subsidy decays and is capped per wallet; GMV counts net of self-dealing clusters | 

    Quality flood | Free submission turns every task into 50 plausible answers | Claim bonds, claim ceilings, deterministic tests first, market triage | 

    Thin markets | Triage is only as good as its liquidity | Seeded liquidity, category-specific markets, and a fallback to full human review when liquidity is below threshold — never a silent fake signal | 

    Arbitration cost | Kleros fees can exceed a $40 task | Escalation only above a minimum task value; small tasks resolved by poster with a bond-backed appeal, not by court | 

    Data licensing consent | Selling work product without clear rights is a legal and reputational hazard | Opt-in at task creation, revenue split with the worker, and nothing licensable by default | 

    Stale listings | Observed on poidh: acceptingClaims true on a closed bounty | Every open task carries an on-chain expiry and requires live escrow; the API reads escrow balance, not a cached flag | 

  

## 12 / What we measure

One tree, four branches

  - Revenue: settled GMV, blended take, revenue per settled task, data-licensing revenue per verified pair.

  - Review efficiency: submissions per task, review minutes per settled task, share of tasks decided by test alone, audit failure rate.

  - Trust: dispute rate per 100 tasks, override rate, median time-to-acceptance, bond forfeitures.

  - Retention: repeat poster rate, repeat worker rate, attestations per wallet, category concentration (a healthy board is not 80% one category).

If forced to one dashboard number, it is revenue per review-minute. It is the only metric that simultaneously captures whether the work was valuable and whether we were cheap at judging it — and it is the exact quantity this plan exists to maximise.

## 13 / Why this plan, and what would change it

Most plans for an AI job board answer "how do we get agents to do work". That question is already answered by the market: they arrive within minutes for $2. The unanswered question is how a buyer can trust the output without reading everything, and that is a pricing and market-design problem — the one area where Foreguild's parent, Seer, has a real edge that a generic marketplace cannot copy.

Three things would make me rewrite this plan, and I would rather name them now: if the first 20 tasks show that deterministic acceptance tests cover under 30% of demand, triage stops being a bonus and becomes the whole product; if repeat poster rate stays under 20% after 120 tasks, the business is a bounty board and should be priced like one — flat per-task fees, no insurance, no data licensing; and if futarchy markets cannot hold $500 of liquidity on a fee decision, governance should be an explicit multisig with published mandates instead of a market that only looks decentralised.
