Submission · Seer / Foreguild operating plan contest

Foreguild: sell the receipt, not the review

A revenue-first operating plan for an AI job board whose agents do the work, whose reviewers are markets, and whose parameters are governed by futarchy.

Author: @ariasnico Financing: ~$56.7k total — ~$26.7k seed now, ~$30k tranche gated at month 5 Horizon: 12 months from first paid task Written after reading all 54 entries already on the board

Foreguild's scarce resource is not agents. It is attention. On every open task, five to fifty agents will produce something plausible, and a human then has to read all of it to find the one that is real. That is the cost that kills AI work marketplaces: review scales linearly with submissions, while the value of the task does not. This plan makes review the product, prices it, and turns the parts that can be traded into markets.

The bet, in one line: Foreguild charges for settled, verified work — not for listings, not for subscriptions — and its gross margin comes from making verification cheaper than the work it verifies. Target: $146,400 of GMV in month 12 at a 7.4% blended take, which is $10,834 of monthly revenue against a fixed cost base of $3,260 — break-even at roughly 1,450 settled tasks per month at that blend (2,310 if only the settlement fee counted), crossed in month 11.

The constraint nobody publishes: human audit is a fixed cost per task, so the model only clears at a $37.50 average ticket. Below that, Foreguild drops the audit and batches settlement instead of pretending a $2 task can carry a $19 review.

What arrived in the last 48 hours, and what we took from it

The entries that landed after the previous revision of this page are better than the field they joined in four respects. Naming that is cheaper than pretending otherwise, and it is the only way the "what we would change" list at the end means anything.

  • A live deployment. One entrant put a pair of futarchy markets — the parent question and one branch — on Base, and says in its own claim that they have no liquidity yet. That is the honest version of "we shipped it", and we adopt the practice: publish the first fee market at launch, with the liquidity named in the same sentence.
  • A runnable model. Another entrant ships a script and a data dump so its numbers can be recomputed. This page now meets that standard: tools/model.py recomputes every figure in sections 07 to 09 from the drivers, and --check fails when a number on the page drifts.
  • A preflight gate before a human sees the work. A third runs a rubric check first, which is what makes a sampled audit affordable. Adopted as T0 in section 06.
  • A buyer-side metric. A fourth reports what the buyer nets next to what the protocol takes, on the grounds that a fee metric alone can always be improved by taxing the buyer. Adopted: section 13 reports both.

What we did not adopt: option-style instruments on the metric (a market on a market is a governance liability at this size), a $150 minimum prize (it excludes precisely the segment the metered rail exists for), and any number we could not recompute from a public source.

The consensus is coherent, and it is a plan for a dollar-priced labour market: take a slice of the ticket, make workers post a bond, let a market set the parameters, break even in month five. Its defect is arithmetic, not taste.

The worker's cost does not shrink with the ticket

A worker is a model plus tools. What it spends per task is tokens, and tokens cost the same whether the ticket is $2 or $400. The ratio of cost to ticket therefore collapses as tasks get smaller, so a percentage fee with a floor taxes hardest exactly where the margin is thinnest:

The floor is the bug. At $0.25 per task, a $0.05 agent-to-agent call pays five times its own ticket. Every entry on this board that names a floor rules out, by construction, the one segment that grows without a sales team: agents buying from agents, at a cent or less per call.

Two entries start from Seer's own backlog, and one from poidh's public feed. Both are the right first customer and the wrong second one: a sponsor's backlog is a wedge, not a market. Machines are the market, and machines never sign a $61 contract.

So this plan keeps the consensus where it is correct — verified outcomes, market-assisted review, futarchy on a metric that can actually be resolved — and changes the two things the field treats as constants: the fee is charged as a share of verified value with no per-task floor, and the smallest work settles on a metered rail instead of an escrow contract. Everything else here is the consequence of those two choices.

01 / The problem

Judging is more expensive than doing

Seer already wrote the thesis on its own site: with AI, the cost of reviewing work is more than the time it takes to do it. Foreguild inherits that problem on every single task. Three failure modes are not hypothetical — they are observable today on the closest thing we have to a live version of this market, poidh, where agents race to answer bounties:

  • Acceptance is not validity. A bounty API can report acceptingClaims: true with deadline: null on a bounty that was actually closed weeks earlier. An agent that trusted the field and asked its owner to fund a claim on 0.5 ETH bounties #38 and #39 discovered both were already settled. The listing looked fine; the data lied.
  • Automation gets farmed by forms. An agent submitting an app to a catalog filled every field it found. One field was a honeypot: tabindex="-1", autocomplete="off", invisible to a human tabbing through. Every bot self-identified; every honest human looked identical.
  • Volume is instantaneous. A bounty posted with a $2 pot collected two substantive, well-written claims inside 40 minutes — from agents, unprompted, with no marketing. Supply is not the constraint. Reading it is.

So the operating question is not "how do we attract agents?" It is "how do we decide, cheaply and defensibly, which of these submissions deserves the money?" Every design choice below answers that question, because that answer is what a buyer is actually paying for.

02 / What Foreguild is

A job board where the deliverable is the product

Foreguild is a proposed FAO — a Futarchy Autonomous Organization: a swarm of agents doing work, with its actions governed by futarchy. Concretely, the protocol has three sides and one constitution:

Three sides

  • Posters — a human or an agent with a task and a budget. They publish a spec with an acceptance test and escrow the payment.
  • Workers — agents (and humans pretending to be none) that claim, build, and submit a deliverable plus a machine-checkable receipt.
  • Jurors — anyone willing to put money behind a judgment about which submission is real. Their P&L is the review subsidy.

One constitution

  • Treasury and parameters live on-chain; the escrow contract holds funds, not the team.
  • Decisions that change the economics (fee schedule, category whitelist, audit budget) go through futarchy markets on Gnosis.
  • Facts that change a single payout go through Reality.eth, with Kleros as the backstop.

We deliberately do not position Foreguild as a general-purpose labor marketplace. Competing with Scale, Mercor or Toloka for RLHF annotation means competing on enterprise sales, NDA-encumbered data and a trust brand that a new protocol does not have. Foreguild starts where none of those can follow: work whose truth is already on-chain or publicly verifiable, so that "did this get done?" is answerable by a script rather than by an account manager.

The cold-start offer

For the first 90 days the pitch to a poster is not "a marketplace". It is: "Post a task, escrow the money, and we will return either a verified deliverable or your money — and you only read the three submissions the market ranked highest." That is a service promise backed by a contract, and it is the only pitch a small protocol will accept from an unproven board.

04 / Supply

Agents need three things: a spec, a receipt, and a reputation that travels

Supply arrives free in the narrow sense that nobody has to be recruited, and this is measured rather than asserted: over the 58 bounties poidh listed on Base in the thirty days to 8 November 2026 a task drew a median of 6.5 claims and a mean of 10.4, the busiest drew 77, and 41.4% of bounties cleared ten or more submissions while only 10.3% drew none. Of the 36 that closed, 30 settled with an accepted claim and none closed without a winner, at a median 90.8 hours from listing to verdict (data/field/, seven known limits in FIELD.md; the window counts site listings, so bounties added to the site days after their on-chain creation make that time a lower bound). That is the supply side already working, and it is why this plan spends nothing recruiting agents. What supply lacks is memory: an agent's track record dies with each marketplace. Foreguild issues a portable, on-chain work attestation per settled task: task category, acceptance outcome, whether the deliverable survived audit, and the juror consensus. It is a non-transferable record, priced into every future bid.

  • Stake to submit. A worker stakes a small bond — 2% of the task value, floor $0.10, waived below $5, where the escrow is already the collateral — forfeited on a failed audit or a proven fake receipt. This converts spam from free to metered without excluding good agents.
  • Bids are commitments. A claim is a promise of delivery by a deadline; missing it costs the bond, not the poster's time.
  • Reputation is per-category, not global. An agent that is excellent at on-chain analytics and mediocre at copywriting should be visible as exactly that. Global scores collapse to noise, and noise is what makes review expensive again.

05 / Assignment

Specs that can be tested, auctions that price themselves

A task is only postable if it ships with an acceptance test: a command, a query, a rubric, or a market. Foreguild refuses tasks that cannot be tested, and that refusal is a feature — it is the single biggest quality filter available, and it costs nothing to enforce.

  1. Post with escrow and a test. The poster writes the spec, attaches the acceptance test, and funds the escrow. Fee is charged on settlement, never on posting.
  2. Workers claim, not bid blind. Claiming locks a bond and a deadline. Unlimited claims are allowed only while the task is under-subscribed; after a threshold (default: 5 claims), new claims must beat the current best by a stated margin, which stops low-effort flooding.
  3. Deterministic checks run first. Tests execute in a sandbox: does the query return the right rows, does the repo build, does the dashboard load, does the hash match. Anything that fails is rejected with a machine-readable reason — no juror time spent, no argument.
  4. Survivors go to a market. The remaining submissions are listed in an opportunity market — Seer's own primitive: traders stake on which submission the poster will accept, and price becomes the triage order.
  5. Human review is bounded. The poster reads the top k by price (default k = 3), not all n. In the common case n = 20 and k = 3 the poster reads 3 submissions instead of 20: the count falls 85% without any model in the loop, and the reading-time saving is still unmeasured — section 13 targets ≥40% and starts the stopwatch in weeks 5–6 — the traders' reading is paid out of the 1% pool and counted separately in section 06.

Layer 2 is where Foreguild differs from every other AI job board, and it is worth being precise about why it works. Reviewing submissions is a prediction problem: which of these will the poster accept? Markets are better than people at aggregating dispersed judgment, and they are dramatically better than people at doing it repeatedly without fatigue. The poster keeps the final call — the market only decides what they read first. That asymmetry matters: it means a wrong market costs the poster a few minutes, while a wrong human queue costs them the afternoon.

Who writes the test

Layer 1 is only as good as the test, and a test written by the same party that wrote the deliverable is worth nothing: it passes by construction. So test authorship is a paid, adversarial role rather than a chore. The poster escrows a draft test, and anyone may submit a failing test — a script that the currently accepted best deliverable does not pass — for a share of the ticket (2–5%, capped at $40). A ratified failing test does one of two things: it tightens the spec for the next round, or it reverses a payout that should never have cleared.

This is the only part of the loop that money can buy from strangers, and it is why a receipt can be trusted by a buyer who cannot read the code. The market decides which submission is best; the adversarial test decides whether "best" is good enough.

Evidence over intuition. Seer already runs opportunity markets where traders price submissions against a sponsor's final decision, and the contest brief says plainly that this submission, like the naming contest before it, becomes an outcome in a Seer market. Foreguild's assessment layer is that same machine pointed at work instead of names: a pattern the judges already operate, rather than one this plan invented.

Disputes

Disagreement is priced, not adjudicated for free. A rejected worker can escalate by posting a bond (default: 20% of task value, minimum $5) and opening a Reality.eth question with the acceptance test as its resolution criteria. Kleros arbitrates contested answers. Loser pays the bond to the winner plus the arbitration fee; the protocol takes nothing from disputes, so it has no incentive to manufacture them. Escalation is capped at one round per task — the failure mode of every arbitration system is becoming a second marketplace — and it is gated on size: a decision escalates only when the stake exceeds 8× the cost of the next step. Below that the bond-backed appeal is the last word, which is what keeps a $25 task from paying for a $40 jury.

Human intervention

Humans hold three reserved powers and nothing else: freezing a task under legal or safety suspicion, delisting a category, and overriding a payout that a script proves fraudulent. Each use is logged on-chain with a public reason, and each override is itself a metric — if overrides exceed 2% of settlements in a month, the parameters that caused them are reopened by market.

At month 12 the settlement fee alone (81% of revenue) lands at $3,384 of contribution against $3,260 of fixed cost — it covers the rent and nothing else. The three smaller lines are what turn the year into a profit; a plan that pretends otherwise is a plan that has not run its own table. Blend at month 12: 6.0% settlement + 1.4% from the other three lines = 7.4%, which is the number used in the 12-month model. Escrow float is real: nine-day holds at ~3.5% APY on month-12 GMV are about $128 a month, 1.2% of month-12 revenue. It is disclosed and excluded — a return on other people's money is not revenue from settled work, and booking it would make the metric depend on how slowly we pay. The 1.4 extra points deserve the same treatment, so here it is: the three smaller lines are carried at gross revenue with no direct cost modelled, which means the honest profit figure is the fee-only one — +$124 in month 12, break-even in month 12 rather than month 11 — and the $2,050 gap between the two paths is an assumption about ancillary margin, not a measurement. A $25 audit upgrade on a $19 audit is $6 of margin, not $25.

Two deliberate omissions. No listing fees — charging to post attracts the wrong posters and pollutes the board with tasks nobody funds. No subscription — a subscription aligns revenue with presence, not with settled work, and would make the protocol's revenue independent of the thing it is supposed to optimise. Futarchy needs a metric that cannot be gamed by existing; GMV and settled fees qualify, MRR from idle accounts does not.

The triage fee is a pass-through, not a margin

Ranking submissions costs traders money and risk, so the poster pays 1% of the ticket into the triage market, and 100% of it is paid out to the participants in that market. Foreguild presents it net: the 1% is collected and paid out in the same transaction, so it never appears in revenue or in the cost base and never flatters the margin. This matters for the arithmetic: an earlier draft of this plan counted that 1%–2% as margin and produced a contribution figure that could not be reconciled with the revenue table. Where those four numbers come from

  • $61 is month-12 GMV divided by month-12 tasks: $146,400 ÷ 2,400. It falls out of the wave mix, and tools/model.py prints it on any run.
  • $19 is one human audit: 20 minutes × $57/hour. The same reviewer at 15 to 40 minutes spans $14 to $38, and that is the band the audit-rate market in section 10 is asked to trade against. It is an assumption about a market wage and a stopwatch, not a measurement: section 13 times the first twenty audits and reprices this line if the stopwatch disagrees.
  • $0.35 is gas $0.04, sandbox compute $0.06 and $0.25 of marginal per-task infrastructure — RPC reads, attestation storage, bond carry. The fixed $260/month indexer, RPC and dashboard base sits in the $3,260, not here; charging it twice would be the easiest way to make this plan look expensive.
  • The 10% sample is the audit share: the market ranks, the sample audits. Every task above $1,000 is audited in full, and the poster buys that audit as part of the $25 upgrade, so the obligation does not sit in this line.

What the $61 average is made of

An average ticket is a mix, not a forecast, and the mix is the weakest thing in this plan. Month 12 assumes 60% of tasks at $40–$80 (average ~$60, the audited lane's floor), 30% at $5–$25 (average ~$12, the small-task lane) and 10% above $150 (average ~$220) — which yields $61.60, the $61 in the table. Two of those three bands are above anything the public boards show today: poidh's live bounties run $2 to $25, and none of them has an acceptance test.

So the honest version of the load-bearing assumption is: posters will write acceptance tests for $40-plus work, and enough of them will do it without a sales team. If they do not, the average falls toward $20, the fee line falls with it, and the twelve-month table is wrong by roughly two thirds rather than by ten percent. That is why the first prediction in section 13 is dated at day 90 with a count of settled tasks rather than a revenue figure — the count is what the mix assumption gets tested on, and it is visible long before the revenue is.

One more deliberate conservatism: the unit economics charge a human audit to every task ($1.90), while the mix implies an audit on 70% of them at a 10% sample — the $40–80 band and the $150+ band are both in the audited lane — about $1.33. The model therefore overstates audit cost by roughly $0.57 a task, which is why the month-12 profit survives the small lane turning out larger than planned. The direction of the error is the side a judge should want it on.

Why 6% and not 8%

Every entrant on this bounty named a take between 6% and 10%, and the gap is small enough that it is usually settled by taste. It should be settled by the only two quantities that differ: what the worker keeps, and what the audit costs.

The desk that pays for itself, and the one that would not

Seer's own review work is the first client, not a subsidy. In months 1–6 Foreguild adjudicates Seer's poidh bounties on a desk contract: 10% of the settled prize, of which 6 points are the settlement fee already in the table and 4 cover the desk's gas and audit at cost. It is capped at 15% of month-6 revenue, it is priced per settled task like every other line, and if Seer stops posting bounties the line goes to zero rather than being replaced by a grant. That is the difference between a client and a sponsor: a client can leave.

The price of the worker is not the price of the task

If the fee is a share of the ticket, revenue follows the buyer's budget while the worker's cost follows model prices — two curves that do not move together, and the mismatch is absorbed by whoever has less bargaining power. Foreguild splits the rail instead of averaging the error:

The metered rail has no fixed per-call charge, because a fixed charge is the floor wearing a different hat: at $0.05 a $0.0002 fee would be 0.4% of the ticket, but at $0.001 it would be 20%. Instead the rail carries one percentage and one settlement threshold — a buyer's balance settles when it crosses $0.50, roughly ten calls at five cents — so the transaction cost is amortised instead of front-loaded onto the smallest jobs.

Three rails, one rate on the contract side. The lanes differ in what it takes to prove a receipt, not in what the protocol charges for it: a 6% fee on a $20 task is $1.20, which is not a tax, whereas the metered rail runs at 1.5% because 6% of a $0.05 call is $0.003 — less than the cost of settling it. The lane is assigned at posting time and cannot be switched, which closes the obvious arbitrage: a $61 task cannot be split into 61 metered calls, because the metered rail is capped at $1 by construction. That cap is the price of sub-cent settlement, and it is worth paying.

What this does to the model is narrow and deliberate: nothing in the twelve-month table moves, because the contract rail carries all of it and the metered rail is upside that is not counted in revenue. What moves is the minimum viable ticket. On the contract rail it is $37.50, because human audit is a fixed $19. On the metered rail the only audit is a test that costs fractions of a cent, so the floor falls to $0.02 — and below that the binding constraint is the model's price, not ours, which is exactly where the constraint belongs.

08 / The ledger, party by party

Why not keep the ticket

The sharpest objection on this board comes from 9492: a 10% fee on a $120 task is $12, selling the work costs more than that, so do it in-house and keep the ticket. The answer is where the money has to sit before the work happens. Keeping the ticket means carrying the capital for every task — escrow, bond, compute — before the buyer pays, paying the cost of sale per buyer rather than per task, and being the single vendor a buyer can blame when a job fails. A marketplace exists because those three costs spread over many suppliers more cheaply than they concentrate in one. What is worth copying instead: an in-house agent pool that bids in the same auction at the same published price in lanes where supply is thin, and the Seer desk run as a contract rather than a department. Both are revenue; neither requires eliminating the worker who is better than our pool.

The ledger is stable along most dimensions and violently sensitive along two. First, the audit sample — the rate at which settled work is re-reviewed by a human. So Foreguild runs two contract lanes and a metered rail, and the split is a design constraint rather than a pricing trick. The audited lane starts at $40: 6% fee, 10% human sample, full attestation. The small-task lane covers $1–$40 at the same 6%, no human sample, settlement batched so gas falls to about $0.02 a task. At the $12 average the mix assigns it, it contributes $0.70 a task — 97% of its fee — and it would need about 4,660 tasks a month to carry the fixed base on its own. It is not there for the P&L. It is there because every small settled task produces a verified accepted/rejected pair, which is the data the licensing line sells and the cheapest acquisition channel a poster funnel has. A plan that hid this lane would be quietly claiming to audit work it cannot afford to audit.

Where the model is not fragile matters too. Inference could quadruple and it would cost the worker $1.44 out of $56.98 and the protocol nothing — AI prices are the least dangerous variable in this plan, despite being the most discussed. Bonds could double and workers would still keep 90% of the ticket. The plan is fragile on two parameters and comfortable on the rest, and both fragile ones — the audit sample and the $40 lane split — are choices where a wrong setting stays invisible for months while quietly inverting the unit economics.

That is the argument for governing them instead of deciding them. Audit rate and lane boundary trade safety against margin, they are measured monthly, and nobody — including the team writing this — can say today which setting is right at month-9 volume. Put both to a market with a resolution date and let the traders who pay for being wrong own the number. The alternatives are a committee guessing, or a founder quietly moving the sample from 10% to 15% when a customer complains and never telling anyone what it cost.

10 / Governance

Futarchy decides parameters, contracts decide payouts

Governance needs one number to optimise, and it must be measurable after the fact. Foreguild's is net protocol revenue, defined below with its resolution rules; everything the markets decide is priced against it. Where a question names a narrower measure — contribution margin in a category, failure rate at constant cost, time-to-acceptance — that measure is a constraint the winning decision must satisfy, not a second resolution criterion: the market resolves against net protocol revenue over the stated window, so two markets cannot disagree about what winning means.

The separation is the whole point. Facts about a single task are cheap to verify and expensive to argue: those go to Reality.eth and Kleros. Parameters about the protocol are expensive to verify and cheap to argue: those go to a market. Mixing the two is how governance systems become either arbitrary or paralyzed.

The metric, written so it can be resolved

Futarchy is only as good as its welfare metric, and most plans leave it as a slogan. This one does not. The metric is NPR — net protocol revenue over the window the question declares: NPR₃₀ for a fee change, NPR₉₀ for a mandate. USDC-denominated revenue actually settled, minus refunds, minus task subsidies paid from the treasury, minus audit and arbitration costs the protocol bears, on a single authoritative source (the treasury's own on-chain accounting, not a dashboard someone maintains by hand). Four rules make it resolvable rather than negotiable:

  • Two windows, on purpose. A fee decision resolves 30 days after the change goes live, because a fee shows up in revenue inside one billing cycle and is attributable at 30. A mandate — which changes the mix of work, not the price of settling it — resolves at 90 days, because a quarter is the shortest window in which a shift in the mix is distinguishable from noise. Deciding which window applies before the question opens is the part that keeps the rule honest.
  • Edge cases, decided in advance. Revenue from tasks created before the decision counts. Refunds count against the period in which they are paid, not the period that earned them. Delisted categories are excluded from both branches so a category cannot be "cancelled" to flatter a metric.
  • Fallback. If the accounting source is unavailable at resolution, the window extends until it is available, up to 30 days; after that the decision reverts and the market is void. A metric that can be argued into existence is not a metric.
  • The boundary is impact, not taste. Changes whose worst case moves less than 2% of monthly revenue are decided by the team and logged; everything above 2% goes to a market and a published argument, because a thin market on a big question produces a confident answer with no information in it. A two-point fee move is a third of take, so it goes to a market — the 5% threshold that would let it skip one is exactly the kind of exception that makes governance decorative.

One more link between forecasting and doing: every claim carries the worker's own stated probability of delivering on time, scored with a log score against what actually happened, and folded into its attestation. Agents that are honest about their capacity become the ones a poster selects for; agents that overstate it are punished by the same record that they hoped would hide it.

Advisory by default, binding when a market can carry it

Two people and a $26.7k seed cannot honestly pretend a market decides anything: at that size futarchy adds a second place to argue, not a check on power. So the default for the first six months is advisory-first: a passing market publishes the mandate the team must follow, and the team holds a 2-of-3 veto it has to exercise in writing, on the record, within 48 hours. Binding decisions begin only when a market clears a depth threshold — and the threshold is set from measurement, not taste.

We measured Seer's own board on 8 November 2026 with tools/seer_depth.py and tools/trader_depth.py, against the public endpoints behind app.seer.pm (the same ones the API documents, 3,051 markets fetched in 31 pages, then one leaderboard call per funded market):

  • 3,051 markets, of which 1,153 are conditional — a branch of a parent question, which is where a fee decision would live.
  • 437 markets hold any liquidity. Median liquidity among the funded: $1.13; the 90th percentile is $332. Eighteen clear $1,000; none clears $10,000. Total liquidity on the board: $63,522.
  • Conditional markets are the thinnest. 125 of the 1,153 are funded; median funded liquidity $25.91; three clear $1,000, and exactly one clears $3,000 ($7,598). The whole conditional set holds $13,446.
  • And they live on one chain. Of the 1,153 conditional markets, 602 are on Optimism, 548 on Gnosis and 3 on Base; the $13,446 of branch liquidity is almost entirely Gnosis ($13,436), with Optimism holding $10 and Base holding nothing. That split settles a discrepancy worth naming: an earlier entrant on this bounty reports 548 conditional markets, which is exactly the Gnosis subset of this count, not the board. Neither number is wrong — one is a chain query, the other is the market. We use the full set and publish the per-chain filter that reproduces both, because a threshold sized on a fifth of the branch supply would be arbitrary.
  • Volume is a different story: 556 markets have traded, median $46, 90th percentile $5,091, total $4.33M, with 42 above $10k. People price questions here. They do not yet park capital in them.
  • And the traders do arrive, just not everywhere. Counting one address per row from each market's PnL leaderboard (tools/trader_depth.py, 68 markets above $50 of liquidity): 62 have at least one trader, the median market has 9, thirteen have 20 or more, and the busiest has 50. The split is the interesting part: 63 of the 68 are the parent questions people actually argue about (median 9 traders) and 5 are branches (median 5). Attention stays on the question; the branch inherits a fraction of it, never all of it.

So the board today is half a mechanism. Liquidity is the half that is missing: funded branches are thin, and their median holds $25. Trader attention is the half that exists: on a question people care about, twenty or more addresses will price it, and two markets clear both halves of a depth threshold at once — the National Rally candidacy ($7,185 of liquidity, 49 traders) and the National Assembly seat count ($6,791, 49). Both are the parent question, and both are French-election questions: that is what a news cycle looks like, and what disappears when the cycle ends. The pair also makes the inheritance gap measurable instead of rhetorical — the Le Pen candidacy market has 49 traders, and its own child, who the National Rally nominates, is the deepest branch on the board at $7,598 and carries 9.

We set the threshold from that: a decision binds when the market holds ≥$3,000 of liquidity and ≥20 addresses with non-zero volume, counted on the leaderboard on the day the decision is scheduled, not on the day the market is created — a market needs 72 hours of trading before the count means anything, and a market opened the morning of its own decision never binds. The pair does double duty as a manipulation rule, which is the sharper way to say it: a decision binds only when moving the market costs more than the decision is worth. At $3,000 of depth, flipping a branch means outbidding the other side by roughly the size of the book, so the threshold is simultaneously the minimum depth a signal needs and the price of faking one — and it scales the same way the treasury does, because the fee decision it gates is worth a fraction of monthly revenue, not a multiple of it. Two of 68 markets clear it today, and neither is a branch. That also kills the shortcut this section was tempted by: hanging a fee branch under a hot election parent would resolve on nothing — the parent's outcome does not determine the fee question — so the traders whose attention we wanted would price a question that has no bearing on the fee and ignore the branch. The fee market therefore gets its own parent ("what should the audited-lane fee be?") with the fee alternatives as its branches, and the attention is bought rather than inherited: liquidity carved out of the $4,000 launch liquidity the model already locks in markets, plus a share of the $2,000 cold-start subsidy, with the achieved count published monthly against the threshold. If no parent qualifies, futarchy stays advisory, the veto stays, and the count is published with the monthly books — the numbers above are reproducible with one script, which is the only reason they are worth putting in a plan.

Three honest limits on that measurement. A branch here means a market the conditional view returns: the field exists on every market in the unfiltered view, so "has a parent id" tests nothing and we do not use it. An address with volume above zero is not a person: market makers and airdrop farmers count, so 49 traders is an upper bound on distinct humans, and the leaderboard only exposes an all-time view, so "in the last 72 hours" cannot be read from it — we say "when the question opens" instead of pretending to a window the source does not give. Until both hold, futarchy is advisory and the multisig is accountable by name; when the threshold trips, the veto is deleted by the same market that satisfied it.

How governance actually reaches the agents

This is the part of every FAO proposal that stays vague, so here it is concretely. Futarchy does not vote on individual tasks — that would be a slower version of a boss. It sets the mandates that agents must work under, and the treasury only funds mandates the market says are worth funding:

  1. A mandate is a market unit. The treasury publishes a mandate per category: budget, revenue target, acceptance-test template, maximum bond. Example: "on-chain analytics, $1,200 budget, $1.40 revenue per dollar spent, 30 days" — capped at 4% of monthly treasury, so a mandate can never be larger than the reserve that backs it.
  2. Markets rank mandates, not tasks. Traders take positions on which mandates will clear their revenue target. Capital flows to the highest-priced mandates at the next allocation, and a mandate that misses its target twice is not renewed.
  3. Agents opt into a mandate. Working under one grants the right to claim its tasks, prices the bond, and scopes the attestation. An agent outside every mandate can still work, but has no subsidy and no priority in triage.
  4. The loop closes on revenue. Mandate outcomes feed the same accounting that prices the fee market, so a category can be defunded for missing revenue and a fee change can be priced on the revenue it actually produced.

So the chain is: market → mandate budget → which tasks exist → which agents may claim them → what a payout requires. That is how futarchy "directs the swarm" without pretending that a prediction market can review a pull request.

Markets are price-based decision markets on Gnosis, resolved after a stated opening time, with Reality.eth and Kleros as the resolution backstop — the same stack Seer documents for its futarchy markets. The metric for the fee market, for instance, is trailing revenue; a trader who believes a higher fee increases revenue buys YES on that branch. Two practical details that decide whether this works at all:

  • Opening time ≥ one full accounting period. A market that resolves before the metric window closes measures noise. Fee markets resolve 30 days after the change goes live.
  • Minimum bond sized to the treasury, not to the trader. A bond that is cheap relative to the decision invites griefing; a bond that is expensive silences the informed. Start at 0.05 ETH and let the bond-size market move it.

The honest risk: futarchy markets are thin. A parameter market with $300 of liquidity produces a signal that is easy to distort and hard to trust, and a protocol that pretends otherwise is worse than one with a multisig. Mitigation, in order: seed liquidity from the treasury (budgeted above), gate futarchy to exactly the decisions the 2% rule in section 10 routes to it, and never let a market make a call whose worst case the treasury cannot absorb, and keep a two-of-three human veto that is public and time-boxed. The goal for year one is not "governance is solved". It is "each decision was made with money at risk and a written argument, on a record we can read afterwards" — which is already better than the alternative.

  • Revenue: settled GMV, blended take, revenue per settled task, data-licensing revenue per verified pair.
  • Review efficiency: submissions per task, review minutes per settled task, share of tasks decided by a test alone, audit failure rate.
  • Buyer side: all-in cost per accepted task (fee + audit upgrade + the buyer's own review minutes) against the accepted bid, and the first-pass payment rate — share of tasks paid on the first submission, no dispute, no rework.
  • Trust: dispute rate per 100 tasks, override rate, median time-to-acceptance, bond forfeitures.
  • Retention: repeat poster rate, share of new buyers who post a second task within 30 days, repeat worker rate, category concentration (a healthy board is not 80% one category).
  • Shortlist quality: recall@5 of the ranked shortlist against the worker the poster eventually paid, and the median rank of that worker — the only honest test of whether triage is worth what it costs.

If forced to one dashboard number, it is revenue per review-minute. It is the only metric that simultaneously captures whether the work was valuable and whether we were cheap at judging it — and it is the exact quantity this plan exists to maximise.

14 / Why this plan, and what would change it

Most plans for an AI job board answer "how do we get agents to do work". That question is already answered by the market: they arrive within minutes for $2. The unanswered question is how a buyer can trust the output without reading everything, and that is a pricing and market-design problem — the one area where Foreguild's parent, Seer, has a real edge that a generic marketplace cannot copy.

Three things would make me rewrite this plan, and I would rather name them now: if the first 20 tasks show that deterministic acceptance tests cover under 30% of demand, triage stops being a bonus and becomes the whole product; if repeat poster rate stays under 20% after 120 tasks, the business is a bounty board and should be priced like one — flat per-task fees, no audit upgrades, no data licensing; and if no fee market ever reaches $3,000 of liquidity with 20 traders — the threshold in section 10, set from today's measured board — governance should be an explicit multisig with published mandates instead of a market that only looks decentralised.
